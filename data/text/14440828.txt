Listening Hour / Public Comment Process - Metro District Research
Purpose and Methodology
Ahead of our upcoming work session on the Listening Hour process, I reviewed board policies, meeting agendas, and district websites for all 56 public school districts in the seven-county Twin Cities metro area to catalog how each structures its public comment / listening session process - format, timing, time limits, sign-up requirements, and livestream status. That data is compiled in the attached spreadsheet.
One important caveat: this is based on published policies and websites, and I have not validated every row against actual practice. To close that gap, I've started watching recordings of real board meetings district by district - as of this summary I've gone through nearly 20 of the other 55 districts, and my goal is to finish the rest before the work session. I've also emailed a handful of board chairs to ask about lessons learned with livestreaming; no one has responded yet, but I'll fold in anything I hear before we meet.
Key Findings
Livestreaming is the norm, not the exception
● 40 of 56 metro districts (about 71%) currently livestream and/or record their listening hour, public comment, or public forum segment.
Where it sits in the meeting
● Most districts that livestream place the comment period directly inside the regular board meeting agenda - typically right after the agenda itself is approved - rather than as a separate, untelevised event beforehand. If no one signs up, or fewer people sign up than the allotted slots, the board simply moves on to business.
Time limits
● 3 minutes per speaker is by far the most common limit. A handful of districts use something different -
2, 2.5, or 5 minutes.
● 30 minutes or 10 speakers is the typical cap on the whole segment, with the chair usually empowered to extend it - in practice, most often from 10 up to 15 speakers. A few districts set a hard cap with no extension allowed.
Speaker order and prioritization
● A recurring pattern, though not a majority: students, then staff, then parents, then community members, then others.
● A more common filter: whether the comment relates to an item already on that meeting's agenda. Onagenda speakers are frequently moved up ahead of general or off-agenda comments.
No camera opt-out where livestreamed
● Where a district livestreams, none appear to offer a formal option to be heard but not shown on camera.
The workarounds people use are having someone else read their statement for them, or submitting comments by email instead of speaking in person. There was one district that only shared audio during the listening period.
Voicemail - available, rarely used
● A small number of districts allow a voicemail submission that's played into the record, but usage appears low based on what I've observed so far.

Reading statements for absent speakers
● In some districts, the clerk or another staff member will read a submitted statement into the record if the person who signed up isn't present. That appears to be very rare.
A problem-and-solution framing, in some cases
● A subset of districts structure their sign-up or comment process to ask not just "what is the problem," but also "do you have a suggested resolution" - building a problem-solving orientation into the format itself.
This is not a requirement for these districts, but rather simply information that is built into the sign up form to promote this type of behavior.
Advance sign-up is the majority approach
● About half of the districts require sign-up before the meeting rather than allowing walk-in, day-of registration. I haven't been able to determine the reasoning behind this from public materials alone. This requirement appears in both institutions that do and do-not live stream their sessions.
Misinformation
● No district appears to have a specific written policy addressing misinformation during public comment. The tool available to every board is the same one we already have - the chair's general authority to rule a speaker out of order or end their time for a decorum violation, not anything misinformation-specific. I will say, of the board meetings I've watched, I have yet to see anything that I
believe would be egregious or way out of line.
Chair or superintendent response time
● A small minority of districts build in response time after public comment concludes. In the sessions I
watched, this was mostly a general thank-you; in a couple of cases the superintendent addressed a specific comment directly, said they'd look into it, or noted they'd route it to the responsible staff member.
